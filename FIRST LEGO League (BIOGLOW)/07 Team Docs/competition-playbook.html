Working groups are not match-day jobs

The groups above are for building the season. On competition day everyone switches to the table roles below and the home-area assignments in section 05, where Rule 11 applies: exactly two technicians per home area, sides locked for the whole match, bench members may only swap in on their own side.

The four pit jobs are real jobs

Attachment runner
Mohamed · Red

Lays out every attachment in run order before the match starts, hands the next one forward the moment the robot is home, and takes the used one back. Nobody hunts for a part mid-match.

Field checker
Husayn · Red

Walks the field before every match and confirms the mission models we depend on are intact and correctly placed. The rulebook is explicit: decorative pieces go missing, and it is our job to alert the referee before the match, not after.

Scorekeeper
Mahdi · Blue

Tracks what actually scored, live. When the referee walks the scoresheet afterwards, Mahdi is the one who can agree or politely disagree with a specific line. Rule 25: once we agree, it is final.

Spotter / timer
Ammar · Blue

Calls the clock out loud at 2:00, 1:00, 0:30 and 0:10 so the technicians know whether there is time for one more launch. Also watches the shared Humongous Fungus interaction with the opposing team.

Match strategy follows from the scoring rule

Only the best of three counts. So match 1 is not a warm-up — it is insurance. We run the known-good program, take the guaranteed points, and give ourselves permission to gamble in match 3.

05

Robot game procedure

This is the choreography. Rehearse it as a sequence, on a timer, with the same four people in the same four positions, until it runs without anybody speaking.

Step 1 · Pre-match inspection 20 points

The referee checks that the robot and every piece of equipment fit inside the two launch areas under a 12 in. (305 mm) height limit. If it all fits in one launch area, we get 20 points. Abbas and Haider run the pack-down together; it should take under thirty seconds and be identical every time.

Step 2 · Setup — a couple of minutes

  1. Distribute the loose mission models provided in home, and our equipment, between the two home areas.
  2. Place the robot in the launch area we are starting from.
  3. Calibrate sensors using any part of the mat — do this every match, venue lighting varies.
  4. Husayn asks the referee to check anything that looks wrong on the field.
  5. Everyone not a technician steps behind the line, coach included.

Step 3 · Positions

Red home left

At the table: Abbas (lead) and Ali.
Behind the line, same side: Mohamed, Husayn.

Mohamed may swap in for either technician at any time — but only on the red side.

Blue home right

At the table: Haider (lead) and Elias.
Behind the line, same side: Mahdi, Ammar.

Mahdi may swap in for either technician at any time — but only on the blue side.

Step 4 · The launch loop

Repeat for every run2:30 total
Runner
Places the next attachment. Robot and anything it is about to move must fit completely inside the launch area.
Technician
Launches. Hands off the moment it moves — no guiding, no nudging.
Both
Watch, hands visibly back. Let the robot come completely home before touching it.
Spotter
Calls the clock: 2:00, 1:00, 0:30, 0:10.
Scorekeeper
Marks what scored on the printed scoresheet as it happens.

Step 5 · Precision tokens — the discipline that wins matches

We start with six tokens worth 50 points. A referee removes one every time we interrupt the robot while it, or anything touching it, is outside home.

Teach the team this one line

The first interruption outside home is free. The second one costs fifteen points — more than Mission 11 is worth. So: never grab the robot out of impatience on the second one. Wait the extra two seconds for it to come home.

If we do have to interrupt

  • Partly outside home — the robot and anything it touched go back to that same home area.
  • Completely outside home — we may place them in either home area.
  • We keep the robot and anything we originally launched with. Anything picked up outside home after the last launch goes to the referee for the rest of the match.
  • Exception worth knowing: if we are not going to launch again, we may stop the robot in place and lose no token. Leave it exactly where it stopped.

Things that void points

  • Handing anything from one home area to the other.
  • Touching anything outside our own home, except to interrupt the robot.
  • Interrupting the robot in a way that earns us points.
  • Separating the Dual Lock, taking a model apart, or breaking a model.
  • Touching the opposing team's field, robot or equipment. Points we cost them score automatically for them.

Step 6 · The buzzer and the scoresheet

  1. At 2:30 the match ends. Stop the robot and touch nothing else. Scoring starts at that instant, and everything must be visibly true at that moment.
  2. The referee walks the results with us. Mahdi compares against his live sheet.
  3. Once we agree, it is official. If something looks wrong, say so politely and specifically before agreeing — after that only the head referee can change it.
  4. Thank the referee and the opposing team. Every match starts at Gracious Professionalism 3; above-and-beyond sportsmanship earns a 4, and those points go into our Core Values total.

Rehearse the failure case

Practise a judge asking something nobody prepared for. The correct answer is "We haven't figured that out yet, but here's how we'd approach it" — said confidently. Guessing and bluffing scores worse than an honest gap.

Final share and feedback — up to 8 minutes

The team may offer closing thoughts. Then the judges give verbal feedback, and this is the most valuable eight minutes of the scrimmage: it tells us exactly what to fix before the qualifier. Elias writes it down — on paper, in the room, while they are speaking. We rebuild both boards from those notes afterwards.

How the scores become a rank

Innovation Project score = all ten Innovation rows added. Robot Design score = all ten Robot Design rows. Core Values score = the five marked rows from each rubric, plus three Gracious Professionalism scores from the matches. Those three scores plus Robot Game become four area ranks, weighted equally; add the four ranks and the lowest total is first in the Champion's rank.

Practical consequence: a strong robot with a weak project cannot win Champion's. All four have to be respectable.

The two rows almost everybody throws away

“Pride or enthusiasm” appears on both rubrics. Two of the ten Core Values rows — twenty per cent of the score — for visibly caring about your own work. It requires no research, no code and no board space. It is lost by mumbling, by trailing off into “yeah, that's it,” and by the five students who are not speaking staring at the floor.

Rehearse the room, not just the script: where each person stands, what they do with their hands, who reacts when a board is pointed at.

Gracious Professionalism is scored three times

Every match starts at Accomplished (3). A referee moves it to Exceeds (4) for above-and-beyond sportsmanship, or down to Developing (2) for less. Those three scores go straight into our Core Values total. Helping the opposing team reset their field, or thanking the referee by name, is worth real points.

One inclusion idea worth stealing

A team Shazia knows made small cards with their team info, attached a sweet to each one, and hand-delivered them to every other team at the event. Other teams loved it, judges saw it, and it cost almost nothing. If we do something like this it has to be genuinely ours and genuinely warm — students designing and handing them out, not a coach's errand.
